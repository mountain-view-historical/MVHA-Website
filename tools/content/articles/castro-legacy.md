Strip malls, expressways, apartment complexes and a misnamed park hide the fact that the area around modern-day Rengstorff Park is one of the most historic sites in Mountain View.

This area was once the heart of the vast Rancho Pastoria de las Borregas — ranch of the ewe’s pasture — an 8,800-acre land grant given to Francisco Estrada by the newly formed Mexican government in 1842. Francisco and his wife Inez Castro built the first known permanent residence in what is now Mountain View in the early 1840s. Their small adobe house stood near what is now Central Expressway and Rengstorff Avenue.

## The Castro family

The Estradas both met early deaths — Inez in 1844 and Francisco in 1845. The couple had no heirs, so the title to their rancho passed to Inez’s father, Mariano Castro. In 1849, Mariano sold the eastern half of the rancho to Irish immigrant Martin Murphy Jr. Murphy’s portion later developed into the city of Sunnyvale.

Around 1850, Mariano replaced the small Estrada adobe with a new house that combined the old adobe style of building with wood construction, featuring a large porch facing west. Mariano lived in the home and raised livestock on the rancho until he became very ill and moved to his other home in San Jose.

## Losing the land

The end of the Mexican–American War, the transfer of California to the United States in 1848, and the Gold Rush of 1849 brought a sudden influx of newcomers to the region. Many of these settlers squatted on the Castro family’s rancho. From 1852 to 1871 the family fought a protracted legal battle to secure title to their land. In the interim years they sold off large portions of the rancho to pay attorney fees — including the area that became downtown Mountain View, with its main street, Castro Street, named after the family. The Castros also deeded land to civic uses, including the town’s cemetery, now Pioneer Memorial Park.

![1876 map of the Villa Lands of Mountain View](assets/images/Villa-Lands-of-Mountain-View.jpg "An 1876 map of the “Villa Lands of Mountain View”.")

In 1864, the San Francisco–San Jose railroad was routed through the Castros’ rancho land. The family was not compensated until six months after trains began running; they were paid $500 for 17 acres of right-of-way. The railroad company also promised the Castros a flag stop near their home — the family simply had to wave a banner to get a passing train to stop. This was eventually formalised as Castro Station, easily confused with the next stop on the line, Mountain View Station at Castro Street. Castro Station was decommissioned in 2000, replaced by the new San Antonio Station half a mile to the north.

![Castro Station depot, circa 1974, looking north toward Rengstorff Avenue](assets/images/Castro-Station-Depot-Not-MVHA-Photo.jpg "Castro Station, circa 1974, looking north toward Rengstorff Avenue. Image source: wx4.org")

## Villa Francisca

In 1911, Crisanto Castro built a beautiful new Mission-style home for his family. He named it Villa Francisca in honour of his wife, who had passed away in 1907. Villa Francisca became a symbol of the Castro family and of the Mountain View area’s ties to California’s Spanish and Mexican eras.

![Castro children in front of Villa Francisca](assets/images/6.jpg "Susan Castro, Crisanto IV Castro, Mercedes Castro and, at back, Frank “Pancho” Castro in front of Villa Francisca.")
![Five of the Castro children as adults](assets/images/8.jpg "Five of Crisanto and Francisca’s children as adults: Joe, Susan, Andrew, Mercedes and Frank Castro.")

Mercedes Castro was the last member of the family to occupy Villa Francisca. In 1958 she sold the house and the surrounding 23.5 acres — the last vestige of the Castros’ 8,800-acre rancho — to the City of Mountain View. Her hope was that the city would turn the land into a park and Villa Francisca into a history museum or senior centre.

On 3 May 1959, the Mountain View Pioneer and Historical Association and the City of Mountain View dedicated a historical marker at the site of the first Estrada/Castro adobe, at Central Expressway and Rengstorff Avenue. Mercedes Castro was an honoured guest. At the time, Villa Francisca was still standing and awaiting its future.

## A park with the wrong name

Renovating Villa Francisca for a new use was not enthusiastically supported by city officials. In 1961, a suspicious fire damaged the first floor of the villa’s west wing. Rather than repair the damage, the city quickly demolished the entire building. Meanwhile, the surrounding 25 acres were transformed into a park. The city contemplated naming the park in honour of the Castros, but was persuaded by councilman Harry True to name it Mountain View Recreation Center Park.

> I go along that the Castro family was a great family in Mountain View, but this is 1959 and this park belongs to all the people of Mountain View. I would like to see the city’s name on it.
>
> — Councilman Harry True, quoted in the Palo Alto Times, 1959

![Villa Francisca, circa 1960](assets/images/9-1024x682.jpg "The Castro family’s Villa Francisca, circa 1960.")

The name did not last long. In 1970, the park was renamed Rengstorff Park to match its location on Rengstorff Avenue. Ironically, until 1929 Rengstorff Avenue was named Pastoria Avenue — in honour of the Castro family’s Rancho Pastoria de las Borregas.

Rengstorff Avenue is named for German pioneer Henry Rengstorff, whose restored house is now a city museum and arguably Mountain View’s best-known historic landmark, in Shoreline Park. Today many people incorrectly assume that Henry Rengstorff once owned the land now occupied by Rengstorff Park. The demolition of Villa Francisca erased the most prominent reminder of the Castro family’s presence here.

![Rengstorff Avenue in 1966, showing Rengstorff Park on the left and Castro City on the right](assets/images/10.jpg "Rengstorff Avenue in 1966, showing Rengstorff Park on the left and Castro City on the right.")

## Castro City

The Castro family name lives on across the street from their former homestead, in the small historic neighbourhood known as Castro City. It was originally subdivided in 1908 and dubbed “University Park” by land speculators who hoped university professors and San Franciscans would buy lots and build country cottages along streets with Stanford-inspired names such as Leland Avenue and College Avenue. Instead the neighbourhood evolved into an affordable, predominantly Latino enclave by the 1950s. The Castro City Market and gas station, run by the Nakamura family, was a neighbourhood landmark until 1992; the building is now home to La Plaza Market.

![Postcard showing a Mountain View residence believed to be in Castro City](assets/images/14-1024x645.jpg "A postcard showing a Mountain View residence, believed to be in Castro City.")
![Castro City Market](assets/images/16.jpg "Castro City Market.")

The name also lives on at nearby Mariano Castro Elementary School and adjacent Castro Park. When the school opened in 1948 it was named Escuela Avenue School, after its location. “Escuela” means school in Spanish, and in 1957 local residents with a grasp of the language realised that “School Avenue School” was a bit ridiculous and petitioned for a change. The name was changed to honour Mariano Castro in 1959.

![Early photo of Escuela Avenue School, now Mariano Castro Elementary School](assets/images/17.jpg "An early photograph of Escuela Avenue School, now Mariano Castro Elementary School.")

## What remains

Today, in Rengstorff Park’s barbecue area, a group of mature eucalyptus and oak trees marks the spot where Villa Francisca once stood. The eucalyptus were likely planted by the Castro family. A sign installed near the barbecue area describes the park’s connection to the Castros and includes a small map of their rancho.
